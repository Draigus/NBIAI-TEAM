# Scope Management: "Put It on the List" Not "No" -- Redirect, Don't Refuse

## Key Content

A production leadership technique for managing scope requests from studios without demoralising teams or creating routing-around behaviour.

**The core problem with "no":**
- Saying "no" to feature requests trains teams to route around the process -- they find informal channels, raise it again with different framing, or stop bringing ideas altogether
- Both outcomes are worse than the original scope request

**The redirect model:**
- Instead of rejecting, redirect every request to a managed backlog (scope list / idea board)
- Any idea gets a destination, not just a verdict: greenlight / defer to the list / slot into a named future milestone
- Plan to visibly pick up approximately four deferred items so the team observes that the list is real and ideas do get actioned
- The visibility step is critical -- if the team never sees deferred items resurface, the list is perceived as a bin

**Pillar language as a filter:**
- Once vision pillars are locked, feature requests can be tested against them
- Pillar-based rejection is more credible than production-based rejection: "this conflicts with our design principles" vs. "we don't have capacity"
- Converts scope debate from resource argument to design argument

**Handling passion vs. questions:**
- Scope additions framed as questions ("could we add X?") signal the requester expects rejection; the passion is low
- Scope additions framed as proposals ("we should add X because Y") signal genuine investment; worth a structured case
- Game director coached to stop treating requests as questions and to redirect to the list instead

## Decisions / Insights

- Glen coached game director: stop saying "no" to scope requests; redirect to the backlog -- "no" trains routing-around behaviour, the list preserves passion.
- Glen decided: plan to visibly action approximately four items from the deferred list so the team sees the list is a real mechanism, not a rejection buffer.
- Glen observed: scope additions framed as questions (vs. proposals) signal low conviction; the structured case requirement (problem/solution/rationale/ask) separates surface interest from genuine creative investment.
- Glen and game director concluded: pillar language, once locked, provides a design-based rationale for scope decisions that is more credible and less contentious than capacity-based rationale.

## Context

Solo notes session by studio advisor (Glen) reviewing scope management dynamics at a ~65-person MMO studio during vertical slice production, Jul 1 2026. The discussion arose from a pattern of informal scope requests (downtime rebuild, plugin proposals, feature additions) reaching leadership outside the formal process.

## Applicability

- Relevant when: a production leader is managing scope pressure from a large studio team -- the redirect model (list + visible actioning) is more effective than binary accept/reject.
- Relevant when: advising a game director who is generating resentment by saying no -- reframing their role as "curator of the list" rather than "gatekeeper" changes the team's perception.
- Relevant when: vision pillars have just been locked -- the transition from capacity-based to pillar-based scope decisions should be explicit and communicated to the team.
- Relevant when: a studio's informal proposal volume is high -- the structured case requirement (problem/solution/rationale/ask) is a low-friction filter that self-selects for genuine investment without requiring process overhead.
