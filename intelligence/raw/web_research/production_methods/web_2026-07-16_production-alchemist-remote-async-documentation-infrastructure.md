# Production Alchemist -- Remote Game Production as Documentation Infrastructure

## Key Content (max 200 words)

Rob Sandberg (Production Alchemist newsletter, #26) frames remote game production as a documentation infrastructure problem: "Remote production is not in-person production with a longer commute. The gaps are different." The core argument is that co-located teams obtain coordination infrastructure naturally (overheard conversations, whiteboard sessions, visible body language); distributed teams must build it deliberately.

**Sync vs async decision criteria:**
- Synchronous (real-time) is reserved for: decisions requiring debate and real-time thinking; situations needing emotional attunement; relationship-building.
- Asynchronous (written) handles: status updates; non-urgent questions; decisions with clear owners; any information needing future reference.

"The default-to-meeting reflex is expensive on any team and destructive on a remote one."

**Decision records as production tooling**: After every meeting, a decision record is written (approximately ten minutes) covering: what was decided, why, who owns the follow-through, and what alternatives were considered. Sandberg: "On a distributed team, documentation is infrastructure. The team cannot function without it." Written notes with named action item owners are required after all meetings.

**Timezone cost rotation**: If the same people always absorb suboptimal meeting slots, timezone burden concentrates and becomes a burnout vector. Tracking and actively rotating who absorbs the awkward time slot is framed as a producer responsibility, not a courtesy.

## Decisions / Insights

- "Any information that can travel in writing should, and on a distributed team that rule is not optional" -- this is the operationalisation of documentation-as-infrastructure
- The decision record format (what/why/who/alternatives, ten minutes) is a minimum viable documentation standard; skipping it creates the "what did we decide?" meetings that compound async costs
- The sync/async decision criteria are not about efficiency -- they are about preserving synchronous time for work that genuinely requires it (emotional attunement, creative debate) and protecting it from work that doesn't
- Time zone burden is tracked at the individual level: which person repeatedly takes 6am calls or 10pm calls? Rotating this burden is the producer's active responsibility, not a scheduling system's passive output
- Studios that maintained distributed practices after returning to office showed improved "project memory" (Sandberg's term) and stronger documentation habits -- the remote-first constraint trained a discipline that compounds value over time

## Applicability

Most directly applicable when advising a studio that has grown to 30-60 people distributed across multiple locations and is experiencing coordination failures: "we didn't know that decision had been made," duplicated work, or creative decisions being made again in meetings because no written record existed. The decision record format (ten minutes per meeting, four fields) is a concrete intervention that a producer can implement in the next sprint without tooling changes. The sync/async criteria are useful as a meeting audit: categorise every recurring meeting by whether it genuinely requires real-time presence, and identify which can be converted to async updates.
