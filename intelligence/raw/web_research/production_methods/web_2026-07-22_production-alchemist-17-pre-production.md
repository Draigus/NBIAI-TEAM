# Production Alchemist #17 -- Pre-Production: What the Producer Does Before Production Starts

## Key Content (max 200 words)

Rob Sandberg defines pre-production's purpose as reducing uncertainty before expensive commitments. "The cost of decisions during this phase is conversation; the same decision in full production costs weeks of rework and stakeholder conflict."

The producer's role is not to write the game design document but to own the process producing it -- specifically to identify the downstream dependencies that creative decisions create. Open-world choices affect content volume and team size; progression systems affect data infrastructure. Two documents protect projects through this phase: a Decision Log (what was decided, by whom, when, and why) and an Open Questions List (unresolved items with assumed answers currently driving other decisions).

Prototypes are treated as question-answering instruments, not demonstrations. The producer must identify which questions a prototype answers and -- critically -- which decisions it accidentally locks in.

Full production readiness requires four conditions: core loop validated with target players, key technical risks resolved, team in place, and first milestone acceptance criteria agreed with any publisher or stakeholder. The author's warning is pointed: "a team that enters full production with unresolved design ambiguity will resolve that ambiguity in the middle of an iteration. That's the most expensive place to do it."

## Decisions / Insights

- Pre-production ends at criterion completion, not calendar date. The producer's obligation is to state with specifics when the project is not ready, naming the unresolved questions and unmitigated risks.
- The Open Questions List is as important as the Decision Log -- unresolved questions driving other decisions are the most dangerous form of technical debt in pre-production.
- Before running any prototype, establish: what question does this answer, what decisions follow from the result, and what assumptions will accidentally become permanent if the prototype proceeds.
- Technical decisions (engine, middleware, platform) made without production input create expensive downstream problems -- incompatible licences, non-compliant middleware, pipeline failures.
- Staffing model is derived from the milestone schedule; when the schedule changes, the staffing model must change. Using contractors as substitutes for permanent positions accumulates knowledge-transfer overhead.

## Applicability

Most valuable when NBI is brought in at the start of a project or when a client studio is transitioning from prototype to production and unsure whether they are ready. The four-condition readiness checklist gives NBI a concrete gate to apply and, where conditions are unmet, a defensible argument for why production should not begin. The Open Questions List structure is immediately portable as a client deliverable -- hand it to the producer at the end of the first NBI engagement session and ask them to populate it before the next meeting.
