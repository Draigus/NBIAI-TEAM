# Production Alchemist #14 -- Scope Management and the Art of Cutting

## Key Content (max 200 words)

Rob Sandberg (Production Alchemist) presents scope management as a continuous weekly discipline, not a crisis response. The core argument: "A plan that has drifted from reality becomes a fiction that costs you the ability to see the actual problem." Post-mortems of slipped schedules consistently reveal the same pattern: individually reasonable decisions accumulate to months of hidden work.

The change request process requires three questions for every scope addition: what is the actual time cost, who does the work, and what exits scope to accommodate it. The process must be lightweight (15-minute conversation, single-line log) to be faster than circumventing it.

The MoSCoW framework (Must Have / Should Have / Could Have / Won't Have) surfaces disagreements before crisis. Its value is not categorisation but the conversation it forces: when stakeholders disagree on Must vs Should, that argument happens with full information rather than under deadline pressure. "The teams that hold together through cuts are the ones where the producer told them the truth early and consistently."

When refusing publisher or leadership scope requests, the correct framing is not "that's not possible" but "here is what we can deliver in available time given current capacity and committed features" -- an invitation to a priority conversation anchored in data.

## Decisions / Insights

- Run MoSCoW as a standing 10-minute agenda item in weekly production meetings; a long Won't-Have list by alpha indicates a healthy project with accurate self-knowledge, not a failing one.
- A change request process only works if it is faster than ignoring it -- keep the overhead to a 15-minute conversation and a single log line, otherwise teams route around it.
- When cutting a feature, acknowledge the work quality explicitly before stating the constraint. Teams fracture when cuts feel sudden and unjustified; they hold together when the producer communicated the constraint before the cut was inevitable.
- Dependency mapping makes the true cost of small features visible -- a "quick" feature touching the core loop generates testing, tutorial copy, UI work, and design iterations that are invisible without a map.
- Stakeholder scope arguments divide into two types needing different responses: priority arguments (they want it) require showing trade-offs; capacity arguments (they need it) require reassessing MoSCoW against remaining time.
